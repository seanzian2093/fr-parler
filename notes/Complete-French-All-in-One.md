# Complete French All-in-One

## Chapter 1 - Articles

### The definite article with nouns

All nouns in French have a gender

- `musculine` or `feminine`
  - whether they refer to a person, an animal, a thing or an abstract notion

English has only one definite article `the`, French uses

- `le` for `masculine` nouns
  - e.g., {{le village, village}}, {{le pont, bridge}}
- `la` for `feminine` nouns
  - e.g., {{la ville, city}}, {{la région, region}}
- `les` for plural nouns, both `masculine` and `feminine`
  - e.g., {{les villages}}, {{les villes}}
- `le` and `la` are shortened to `l'` before singular noun or adjective that begins with a vowel sound or a muted **h**
  - `masculine`: {{l'océan, ocean}}, {{l'ami, friend}}
  - `fiminine`: {{l'île, island}}, {{l'autoroute, highway}}


### The indefinite and partitive articles with nouns

Indefinite articles(a, an or some in English) are

- `un` for `masculine` singular noun
  - e.g., {{un projet, a project/plan}}, {{un bâtiment, a building}}
- `une` for `feminine` singular noun
  - e.g, {{une maison, a house}}, {{une lampe, a lamp}}, {{une avalanche, an avalanche}}, {{une aubergine, an eggplant}}
- `une` or `un` for `masculine` and/or `feminie` nouns
  - e.g. {{un(e) architecte, an architect}}, {{un(e) artiste, an artist}}, {{un(e) journaliste, a journalist}}
- `des` for both `masculine` and `feminine` plural
  - e.g., {{des murs, walls}}, {{des balcons, balconies}}

Partitive article is used when exact quantity of an item is unknown

- always required in French
- in English, partitive article is often ommitted
  - `I want bread` instead of `I want some bread`

It is formed by combining `de` and the definite article

- `de` + `le` = `du`
- `de` + `l'` = `de l'`
- `de` + `la` = `de la`
- `de` + `les` = `des`
- `du`, `de la`, or `des` all become `de` in negative since quantity does not exist any longer
  - except when using with verb `etre`, in negative, partitive article is always used

### Accents

- Accent Aigu
  - Acute accent is used only with the vowel **e**. It indicates
  - the vowel should be pronounced as a _closed_ _e_, similar to _e_ in English word _bed_
    - e.g., {{le café, coffee}}, {{le céléri, celery}}
  - In some words, the initial **é** replaces an initial **s**. Latin **s** is still alive in English cognates.
    - {{l'éponge, sponge
}}

- Accent Grave
  - used on the vowels **a**, **e**, and **u**
  - more open pronunciation
    - e.g., **è** resembels the vowel sound in English word **bad**
    - e.g., {{à, at/in}}, {{après, after}}, {{où, where}}

- Accent Circumflex
  - used on **a**, **e**, **i**, **o**, and **u**
  - if the English cognate contains an **s** that is missing in the French word, this French word will need a circumflex accent.
    - e.g., {{le coût, cost}}, {{la forêt, forest}}, {{l'hôpital, hospital}}, {{la pâte}}

- Accent diaeresis, or **tréma**
  - used when two vowels are next to each other and the accented vowel represents a separate sound
    - i.e., both vowel must be articulated.
    - e.g., {{aïe, ouch}}, {{Noël, Christmas}}

- Cedilla, graphic sign, not an accent
  - used under the letter **c**, before vowle **a**, **o**, and **u**
    - not with vowels **e** or **i**
  - it changes the hard sound **k** (like **c** in **catalogue**) to soft sound {{ça}}(**th** in **this**, **that**)
    - e.g., {{la façade, facade/front}}

- h aspiré
  - Some nouns start with a silent **h**, of which
    - the article that precedes some needs an apostrophe, e.g., {{l'habitant, habitant}}
    - the article that precedes some does not need apostrophe, i.e., no liaison, {{la haie, hedge}}
      - this is called **h aspiré**

### Capitalization

Capitalization is more frequent in English than in French

- Nationalities: adjectives that refer to nationalities are not capitalized in French
  - e.g., {{Marie est française, Marie is French
}}

- Languages: languages are not capitalized in French
  - e.g. {{Ils apprennent l'arabe, They are learning Arabic
}}

- Locations: when writing an address, words for street, avenue, place and other names are not capitalized in French
  - e.g., 25, {{rue de l'Université, University Street}}

- Dates, time, days, months, and seasons: such words are not capitalized
  - e.g., {{Nous partirons le 5 décembre, we'll leave on December 5
}}

- Religions: names of religions are not capitalized except **l'Islam**
  - e.g., {{le christianisme, Christianity
}}

- Geographical nouns: Geographical names are capitalized in French.
  - e.g., {{l'Asie, Asia}}, {{l'Europe, Europe}}, {{les États-Unis, United States}}
  - if the geographical noun is composed of a generic name(_bay, cape, river, sea_, etc) and a specific name, only the specific name is capitalized
    - {{le cap Horn, Cape Horn
}}
    - {{la péninsule Ibérique, the Iberian Peninsula
}}
  - Some irregular:
    - {{l'Asie centrale, Central Asia}}, {{l'Asie Mincure, Minor Asia}}

- Addressing people: when writing to people in French, capitalize most titles
  - {{Monsieur Verneuil, Mr Verneuil}}, {{Madame Deneuve, Madam Deneuve}}, {{Monsiuer le Premier ministre, Mr Premier Minister}}

## Chapter 2 - Basic Gender Endings

### Basic Masculine Noun Endings

The following endings **tend** to be masculine

- **-age**, **-ige**, **-ège**, **-oge**, **-uge**
  - e.g., {{le fromage, cheese}}, {{le mariage, marriage/wedding}}
  - exceptions:
    - {{la cage, cage}}, {{la loge, loge/dressing room}}, {{la page, page}}
- **-ail**, **-euil**
  - e.g., {{le fauteuil, armchair}}, {{le seuil, threshold}}
- **-ain**
  - e.g., {{le bain, bath}}, {{le pain, bread}}
  - one exception: {{la main, hand}}
- **-al**
  - e.g., {{le cheval, horse}}, {{le mal, evil}}
- **-ament**, **-ement**
  - e.g., {{le branchement, connection}}, {{le départment, department}}
- **-ard**
  - e.g., {{le canard, duck}}
  - some words ending in **-ard** may have a pejorative connotation
    - e.g., {{le bâtard, bastard}}, {{le chauffard, reckless driver}}, {{le clochard, tramp}}, {{le vantard, braggart}}
- **-eau**, **-ou**
  - e.g., {{le bateau, boat}}, {{le bijou, jewelry}}
  - exceptions:
    - {{la peau, skin}}, {{l'eau, water}}

- **-el**
  - e.g., {{l'appel, call}}, {{le caramel, caramel}}, {{le logiciel, software}}, {{le sel, salt}}

- **-ent**, **-ant**
  - e.g., {{l'argent, money}}, {{le restaurant, restaurant}}
  - exception:
    - {{la dent, tooth}}

- **-er**
  - e.g., {{le boulager, baker}}, {{le déjeuner, lunch}}

- **-ier/yer**
  - e.g., {{le calendrier, calendar}}, {{le loyer, rent}}

- **-at**, **-et**, **-t**

  - e.g., {{le trombinoscope, group photo}}

  - e.g., {{l'agenda, agenda}}, {{le visa, visa}}

  - e.g., {{l'ange, angel}}, {{le beurre, butter}}, {{le pouce, thumb}}, {{le verre, glass}}

### Other Cases

- **-ice**
  - often `masculine`, e.g., {{le dentfrice, toothpaste}}, {{le service, service}}
  - exceptions: {{l'avarice, greed}}, {{l'épice, spice}}, {{la cicatrice, scar}}, {{la malice, malice/mischievousness}}
  - exceptions: `ferminine` forms of `masculine` words to denote people practicing particular professions, e.g., {{la rédactrice, female editor}}, {{la directrice, female director}}

- **-aire**, **-oire**
  - e.g., {{l'anniversaire, birthday}}, {{le dictionnaire, dictionary}}
  - long list of exceptions: {{la victoire, victory}}, {{la grammaire, grammar}}

- **-oi**, **-ois**, **-oix**
  - mostly `masculine`
  - only exceptions: {{la croix, cross}}, {{la foi, faith}}, {{la loi, law}}, {{la noix, nut}}, {{la voix, voice}}, {{la paroi, wall/partition}}

- **-f**, **-aim**
  - mostly `masculine`
  - only exceptions: {{la clef, key}}, {{la clé, key}}, {{la nef, nave}}, {{la faim, hunger}}, {{la soif, thirst}}

- **-ole**, **-ôle**, **-ule**
  - Nouns ending in above seem willing to go either way

- **-iste**, **-que**
  - nouns ending in above that refer to persons can be either `masculine` or `feminine`, depending on context
  - e.g., {{le paysagiste, landscape artist}} or {{la paysagiste, landscape artist}}
  - other nouns ending in **-iste** are `feminine`
    - e.g., {{la liste, list}}, {{la piste, track/path}}

### Nous Derived from Past Participles

- Nouns derived from adjectives and the `masculine` forms of the past participle are `masculine`
  - e.g., {{le bien, good}}, {{le mal, evil}}

- Nouns derived from adjectives and the `feminine` forms of the past participle are `feminine`
  - e.g., {{la belle, the beautiful}}, {{la crue, flood}}

## Chapter 3 - More French Nouns and Their Gender

- As a rule, professions are `masculine` when they refer to a man and `feminine` when they refer to a woman. We can just add a final **e**
  - e.g., {{l'apprenti, male apprentice}} and {{l'apprentie, female apprentice}}, {{l'avocat, male lawyer}} and {{l'avocate, female lawyer}}

- In the past, traditionally `masculine` professions, even if practiced by women, remained `masculine`
  - e.g., {{le compositeur, composer}}, {{le procureur, prosecutor}}, {{le peintre, painter}}, {{le professeur, professor/teacher}}

- Some nouns of professions are identical in `masculine` and `feminine`
  - e.g., {{l'architecte, male architect}} and {{l'architecte, female architect}}
  - e.g., {{le dentiste, male dentist}} and {{la detiste, female dentist}}

- Some nouns are always `masculine` whether rederring to a man or a woman
  - e.g., {{le chef, leader/boss}}, {{le sauveur, savior}}, {{le mannequin, model}}

- Some nouns are always `feminine` whether rederring to a man or a woman
  - e.g., {{la star, star}}, {{l'idole , idol}}, {{la personne, person}}

- Some `feminine` nouns are used only for men
  - e.g., {{la brute, bully/boor}}, {{la crapule, villain/crook}}

- Most names of animals are **épicène**, meaning "common", which works with either gender. To differentiate between the two sexes, we add**mâle** or **femelle**. But these names have their own gender.
  - `masculine` names, e.g., {{le cochon, pig}}, {{le koala}}, {{le oiseau, bird}}
  - `feminine` names, e.g., {{la gazelle, gazelle}}, {{la souris, mouse}}, {{la loutre, otter}}

- Places and the Calendar
  - `masculine` is used more commonly for cities but `feminine` is used in a more aesthetic or romantic sense
  - Names of towns preceded by a `masculine` definite article are `masculine`
    - e.g. {{Le Harre, Le Havre}}, {{Le Caire, Cairo}}
  - Names of towns preceded by a `feminine` definite article are `feminine`
    - e.g., {{La Rochlle, La Rochelle}}, {{La Havana, Havana}}
  - Gender of Italian cities varies, depending on context
    - e.g., {{La Florence des Médicis}}, {{La Florence de James Ivory}}
  - Usually, when the nouns of cities are preceded by the adjectives **grand**, **nouveau**, and **vieux**, they are used in `masculine` form
    - e.g., {{Le Grand Prague, grand Prague}}

  - Countries ending with **-e** tend to be `feminine`, others `masculine`, with exceptions
    - e.g., {{la Chine, China}} {{la France, France}}, {{le Canada, Canada}}
    - exceptions: {{le Mexique, Mexico}}
    - some countries do not have articles: **Israëél**, **Oman**
  
- Days, Months, Seasons, and Cardinal Points
  - Days of the week are `masculine`
    - e.g., {{le lundi, Monday}}, {{le mardi, Tuesday}}
  
  - Months of the year are `masculine`
    - e.g., {{janvier, January}}, {{un avril ensoleillé, a sunny April}}
  
  - Seasons are `masculine`
    - e.g., {{un hiver froid, a cold winter}}, {{un printemps tardif, a late sprint}}, {{un été pourri, a rotten summer}}, {{un automne pluvieux, a rainy autumn/fall}}

  - Cardinal points are `masculine`
    - e.g, {{le nord, north}}, {{le sud, south}}, {{l'est, east}}, {{l'ouest, west}}

- Plants, Wine, and Cheese
  - Most flowers not edning in **-e** tend to be `masculine`
    - e.g., {{le jasmin, jasmine}}, {{le tournesol, sunflower}}
  
  - `feminine` flowers tend to end in **-e**
    - e.g, {{la rose, rose}}, {{la violette, violet}}

  - Most trees and shrubs tend to be `masculine`
    - e.g., {{le chêne, oak}}, {{le cèdre, cedar}}, {{l'érable, maple}}, {le pin, pine}

  - Names of fruit, nuts, and vegetables not ending in **-e** tend to be `masculine`
    - e.g., {{le citron, lemon}}, {{le kiwi, kiwi}}
    - exceptions: {{le concombre, cucumber}}, {{le gingembre, ginger}}, {{le pamplemousse, grapefruit}}
    - names ending with **-e** tend to be `feminine`
      e.g., {{la carotte, carrot}}, {{la cerise, cherry}}, {{la pomme, apple}}
  
  - Names of wines and algoholic beverages tend to be `masculine`
    - e.g., {{le whisky, whisky}}, {{le sake, sake}}
    - exceptions: {{la bière, beer}}, {{la vodka, vodka}}

  - Names of cheese tend to be `masculine`
    - e.g., {{le brie, brie}}, {{le cheddar, cheddar}}, {{le parmesan, parmesan}}
    - exceptions: {{la feta, feta}}, {{la mozzarella, mozzarella}}, {{la ricotta, ricotta}}

- Colors, Fabric, Elements

  - the word **couleur**(color) itself is `feminine` as it ends with **-eur**. The colors themselves are `masculine`
    - e.g., {{le blanc, white}}, {le bleu, blue}, {{le gris, gray}}

  - Most names of fabric are `masculine`
    - e.g., {{le coton, cotton}}, {{le cuir, leather}}
    - exceptions: {{la laine, wool}}, {{la soie, silk}}
  
  - Precious Stones
    - Stones can be either `masculine` or `feminine`
    - e.g., {{le dimant, diamond}}, {{le jade, jade}}
    - e.g., {{la topaze, topaz,}}, {{l'opale, opal}}
  
  - Metals, Minerals, Chemical Elements
    - Most names of metals, minerals, and chemical elements are `masculine`
    - e.g., {{l'argent, silver}}, {{le cuivre, copper}}, {{le sel, salt}}
    - exceptions: {{la chaux, lime}}, {{la roche, rock}}

  - Cars
    - In most cases, cars are used in `feminine` form
    - Some minivans or SUVs use `masculine` form

## Chapter 4 - Numbers
